9 Sustainability Activities for Primary School

Practical investigations that help students notice systems, compare choices, collect evidence and design achievable improvements—without turning sustainability into a guilt lecture.

What are good sustainability activities for primary school?

Good sustainability activities help primary students understand a real system before asking them to act. For ages 6–11, that can mean tracing where rainwater goes, comparing habitat features, following the path of a classroom resource, testing shade, or using evidence to redesign one everyday routine. The strongest activities connect observation, consequences and a realistic next step.

Age6–11 years

How sustainability appears in the Australian Curriculum

In the current Australian Curriculum, Sustainability is a cross-curriculum priority. It emphasises understanding interconnected systems, considering environmental, social and economic impacts, and using informed action to contribute to sustainable futures. The ideas below are practical teaching prompts rather than formal year-level curriculum mappings; teachers should align them with their own learning intentions and local requirements.

9 low-prep sustainability activities

1. School systems map

Choose one familiar area and map the systems that pass through it: water, electricity, food, waste, people, plants and animals. Add arrows where one affects another.

Ask: If one part changed, what else might change with it?

2. Shade and heat comparison

Compare a shaded area with a nearby exposed surface. Students record temperature if equipment is available, or use safe observational evidence such as surface warmth and comfort.

Ask: Where would extra shade make the biggest difference, and to whom?

3. Rainwater route investigation

After rain, safely trace where water pools, flows, enters soil or reaches drains. Do not approach stormwater openings or moving water.

Ask: Which surfaces slow water down, and which move it away quickly?

4. Habitat feature audit

Compare two school-ground areas for plant variety, shelter, shade, leaf litter, flowers and visible animal activity without disturbing habitat.

Ask: Which features seem most useful to living things, based on what you observed?

5. Resource journey

Choose one ordinary classroom item and brainstorm the stages in its journey: materials, making, transport, use and what happens afterwards. Mark any stage where information is unknown.

Ask: Which part of the journey would you want to investigate before making a claim about the “best” choice?

6. Use-it-longer challenge

Choose a non-consumable classroom object. Students design three ways to extend its useful life through maintenance, repair, sharing, repurposing or better storage.

Ask: Which idea saves resources without making the object less useful?

7. Redesign one routine

Pick a small routine such as paper use, lights, lunch waste or classroom materials. Observe the current routine first, then compare two realistic alternatives.

Ask: What trade-offs would each option create for people, cost, convenience and the environment?

8. Biodiversity before-and-after plan

Imagine adding one habitat-supporting feature such as native flowering plants or extra shelter. Before acting, students decide what they would measure now and later to judge whether it helped.

Ask: What evidence would count as improvement?

9. Preferred-future school space

Students redesign one small outdoor area for a future school, balancing shade, habitat, water, movement, accessibility and human use.

Ask: Which needs compete with each other, and how did your design balance them?

A better sustainability sequence: understand → compare → act → check

Avoid beginning with “save the planet.” Start with a system students can observe. Then compare options, choose one proportionate action and decide how they would know whether it worked. This keeps sustainability practical, evidence-based and age-appropriate while still giving children meaningful agency.

Questions teachers ask

Is sustainability just recycling and waste?

No. Sustainability also includes biodiversity, water, energy, design, consumption, systems, wellbeing, resource use and considering how decisions affect the future. Waste is one useful context, but it is not the whole subject.

How do I teach sustainability without frightening children?

Use a local, observable problem and a manageable scale. Ask students to investigate what is happening, compare choices and propose one realistic improvement. Avoid making children responsible for solving global problems they cannot control.

Can sustainability activities work across subjects?

Yes. The Australian Curriculum treats Sustainability as a cross-curriculum priority. A single investigation can involve Science observation, HASS systems thinking, Maths measurement, English explanation or Technologies design depending on the learning intention.

Do students need to complete an environmental action every lesson?

No. Understanding a system and evaluating evidence are valuable learning outcomes. Action is more meaningful when students first understand the problem and can explain why a particular response makes sense.

Can these activities be used at home?

Yes. Most can be simplified for a backyard, apartment complex, local park or homeschool setting by choosing one small place or household routine to investigate.
