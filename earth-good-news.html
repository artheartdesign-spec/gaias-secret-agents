AUSTRALIA • Queensland wildlife

150 bilbies are back in the wild!

150 greater bilbies

After disappearing from Currawinya National Park in the 1970s, greater bilbies have been released back into the park. The Queensland Government reported the September 2026 return after years of breeding, restoring habitat and keeping introduced predators under control.

Why it matters: Bilbies dig for food, helping mix soil. Their return shows how people working together can help a missing species come home.

Ask an agent: Why might a digging animal be important to other living things?

Source: Queensland Government — threatened species news (September 2026).

AUSTRALIA • Koala country

A big step for koala habitat

178,000 hectares

On 23 October, New South Wales announced that legislation had passed to add 178,000 hectares to the national park network for the planned Great Koala National Park. The forests are home to an estimated 12,000 koalas and more than 100 other threatened species.

Why it matters: Animals need connected places to find food, shelter and mates. Legislation is an important step, though the park was still being established when announced.

Ask an agent: What would you put in a perfect home for a koala?

Source: NSW Department of Climate Change, Energy, the Environment and Water (23 October 2026).

WORLD • Nature restoration

People are helping nature make a comeback

252 million hectares

A United Nations report released on 29 October found that restoration is under way across 252 million hectares in 149 countries. That means people are repairing forests, wetlands, rivers and other ecosystems on a huge scale.

The important catch: The UN says the world is still behind on its 2030 restoration goals. Real progress is happening, and more work is needed.

Ask an agent: If you could help restore one habitat, which would it be?

Source: UN Environment Programme (29 October 2026).

AUSTRALIA • Wild places

More room for Queensland’s wild neighbours

Nearly 400,000 hectares

Queensland reported in October that three large properties across Cape York and the Gulf of Carpentaria were secured through a conservation partnership. These places contain wetlands, woodlands, waterholes and habitat for many threatened birds and other animals.

Why it matters: Protecting an entire habitat helps plants, insects, birds and mammals—not just one famous animal.

Ask an agent: How many different homes can you find in one tree?

Source: Queensland Government — threatened species news (October 2026).

This week’s secret mission • 15–20 minutes

Mission: The Tiny Habitat Detective

Your assignment: Discover how a tiny outdoor space can be a whole neighbourhood for living things.

  1. Find a safe patch of garden, lawn, park or schoolyard. Leave animals, nests and plants undisturbed.
  2. Look carefully for three kinds of homes: under leaves, on bark, amongst flowers, or in the soil.
  3. Sketch a mini-map. Label the places offering food, water or shelter.
  4. Choose one small helpful action with an adult: leave some leaf litter in a suitable garden corner, plant a locally appropriate native plant another day, or simply protect the habitat by not disturbing it.

Agent question: “How can a place that looks messy to us be useful to wildlife?”

Safety: supervise children near water, roads and unfamiliar plants; do not touch wildlife, fungi or animal droppings. Wash hands afterward.

Family talk: Would you rather…?

Help a bilby find a safe new home or help a koala find a path through connected trees? Explain your choice, then swap sides and argue for the other animal.

There is no wrong choice. The goal is to listen, imagine and learn together.

Teacher corner: 10-minute classroom starter

Suitable for: Australian primary classrooms (adaptable, especially Years 2–6). Preparation: none; read one sourced story aloud and display or print the prompts.

  • 2 minutes — Notice: Which facts tell us something actually happened?
  • 3 minutes — Think: What does this animal need from its habitat?
  • 3 minutes — Discuss: What is the difference between a conservation promise and a result?
  • 2 minutes — Act: Each student draws one practical way to care for local habitat.

Skills: evidence literacy, habitats and biodiversity, speaking and listening, and informed action. Teachers can align this activity with their year-level curriculum; this is not a formal curriculum mapping.
