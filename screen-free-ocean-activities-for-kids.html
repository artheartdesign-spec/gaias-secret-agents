Ocean Field Guide · Ages 6–11

12 Screen-Free Ocean Activities for Kids

Beach, rock-pool and at-home investigations that turn ocean curiosity into careful observation, simple science and positive action.

Low prepBeach + home optionsGrown-up friendly

You do not need a perfect beach day—or even a nearby beach—to help a child investigate the ocean. Give them one clear objective, a safe way to collect evidence and a question worth bringing home.

Choose the right mission

Activities 1–6 work best at the shore. Activities 7–12 can be completed at home, in a backyard or near any safe outdoor water source. Choose one or two rather than trying to complete the entire list in a day.

Rock-Pool Census

Best at low tide · Observe only

Select one rock pool and count the different kinds of living things you can see without touching or moving them.

  • Look for snails, crabs, small fish, seaweed and anemones.
  • Record shapes and colours when you do not know a species name.
  • Repeat at a second pool and compare the results.

Why it matters: children discover that even a tiny pool can hold a whole community.

Tide-Line Detective

Beach investigation · 15 minutes

Walk parallel to the water along the natural line of shells, seaweed and other material left by the last high tide.

  • Find three natural objects and describe where they may have come from.
  • Count human-made items without collecting anything unsafe.
  • Predict where tomorrow’s tide line might appear.

Why it matters: the tide line reveals how water moves both natural material and litter.

Shorebird Behaviour Watch

Quiet observation · Keep your distance

Choose one shorebird and watch for five minutes. The mission is to record what it does rather than identify its species.

  • Tally pecking, running, flying, resting and calling.
  • Notice whether waves or people change its behaviour.
  • Sketch its beak and feet and consider how each might help it feed.

Why it matters: patient watching develops respect for wildlife and reveals adaptations in action.

Ocean Sound Map

Sit spot · Paper and pencil optional

Sit safely above the waterline. Imagine a dot in the middle of a page represents you, then place each sound around it.

  • Use words, symbols or finger directions.
  • Separate natural sounds from human-made sounds.
  • Choose the quietest sound you could hear.

Why it matters: careful listening slows the experience down and strengthens awareness of place.

Wave Pattern Mission

Dry observation point · No water entry

From a safe dry position, count how many waves reach the shore in one minute.

  • Repeat three times and compare the totals.
  • Notice whether waves arrive alone or in groups.
  • Look for the point where each wave begins to curl or break.

Why it matters: repeated observations introduce data collection without turning the outing into a lesson.

One-Metre Beach Survey

Choose a safe square · Leave it unchanged

Mark an imaginary square about one metre wide on dry sand and investigate everything visible inside it.

  • Count shells, plant pieces, animal tracks and human-made items.
  • Look closely for holes, trails and patterns.
  • Compare a square near vegetation with one closer to the tide line.

Why it matters: limiting the search area helps children notice details they would normally walk past.

Saltwater Float Test

At home · Bowl, water, salt and small objects

Predict whether the same object will float differently in fresh water and salty water.

  • Mix several spoonfuls of salt into one bowl and keep a second bowl fresh.
  • Test a grape, small carrot piece or tightly closed plastic building block.
  • Add salt gradually and record when the result changes.

Why it matters: dissolved salt increases water density, helping some objects float more easily.

Make a Model Current

At home · Clear dish and food colouring

Fill a clear shallow dish with water. Place one drop of colouring at one end and move the water gently with a spoon at the other.

  • Watch the coloured water travel.
  • Try a slow circular movement, then one straight movement.
  • Predict where a floating leaf fragment would go.

Why it matters: a simple model shows how moving water can carry heat, nutrients and pollution.

Ocean Plastic Source Hunt

Home investigation · No beach required

Choose five common plastic items in your home and investigate which ones could escape into drains, streets or waterways.

  • Look for single-use packaging and tiny loose pieces.
  • Choose one item the household could reuse, replace or secure better.
  • Make the change for one week and report back.

Why it matters: ocean protection often begins far from the ocean.

Sea Turtle Navigation Challenge

Backyard or park · Movement mission

Choose a start point as the “feeding ground” and a finish point as the “nesting beach.” One person secretly places three safe obstacles between them.

  • The migrating turtle must find a route without touching an obstacle.
  • Try again with a partner giving only compass directions.
  • Discuss why real turtles need safe migration routes and dark nesting beaches.

Why it matters: movement makes migration challenges easier to imagine and remember.

Design a Wildlife-Safe Beach Sign

Creative field file · Paper and drawing tools

Create one clear sign that helps visitors protect a shore animal or habitat.

  • Choose one action: give birds space, take litter home or keep lights low.
  • Use one strong image and no more than eight words.
  • Test whether another person understands it in five seconds.

Why it matters: designing for a real audience turns knowledge into useful communication.

Family Ocean Action File

Positive action · One week

Choose one realistic household action that protects waterways and track it for seven days.

  • Try a reusable lunch item, a litter-free outing or careful drain disposal.
  • Make one tally mark each time the family completes the action.
  • At the end, decide whether to keep, change or replace the mission.

Why it matters: small repeated actions build agency without frightening or blaming children.

Field safety comes first

Check tides and local conditions before visiting the shore. Supervise children closely around water and slippery rocks. Wear suitable footwear and sun protection. Never handle unknown marine life, remove animals from rock pools, turn over fixed rocks, approach nesting birds or collect living shells. Leave every habitat as you found it.
